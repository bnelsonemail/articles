# How Engineers Become Trusted Advisors

Most engineers are trained to solve problems.

We calculate flows. Develop alternatives. Prepare designs. Evaluate risks. Write specifications. Manage construction.

Those skills are essential.

But they aren't necessarily what makes a client trust you.

Over time, I've come to believe there is an important distinction between being a **technical expert** and becoming a **trusted advisor**.

A technical expert answers the question.

A trusted advisor helps the client determine whether they're asking the right question in the first place.

## IT STARTS WITH UNDERSTANDING THE CLIENT'S WORLD

Municipal infrastructure decisions rarely exist in isolation.

A utility director may be evaluating a pump station upgrade, but the engineering problem is only part of the decision.

They may also be dealing with:

- limited capital funding
- an upcoming budget cycle
- regulatory deadlines
- staffing constraints
- competing projects
- elected officials
- customer expectations
- operational concerns

If we only focus on the technical assignment in front of us, we may produce an excellent design while missing the larger problem the client is trying to solve.

Trusted advisors understand that larger context.

They don't just ask:

**"What do you want us to design?"**

They also ask:

**"What are you trying to accomplish?"**

That difference matters.

## TRUST IS BUILT BETWEEN PROJECTS

It's easy to be responsive when there's an active project.

There's a scope. A schedule. A budget. Meetings on the calendar.

But some of the most valuable client relationships are built when there isn't an immediate project to pursue.

Maybe you share something you learned from another utility.

Maybe you alert a client to a regulatory change.

Maybe you call because you remember they were struggling with an operational issue.

Maybe you connect them with someone who can help, even when your firm won't receive any work from it.

Those interactions demonstrate something important:

**You're paying attention to their success, not just your next contract.**

That is difficult to manufacture through a formal business development process.

It has to be earned.

## SOMETIMES THE BEST ADVICE DOESN'T CREATE A PROJECT

This may be one of the hardest lessons in consulting.

Sometimes the right recommendation is:

**Don't build it yet.**

Maybe rehabilitation makes more sense than replacement.

Maybe additional monitoring would reduce uncertainty.

Maybe an operational change can extend the useful life of an asset.

Maybe another planned project will solve the same problem two years from now.

That recommendation might reduce the immediate engineering fee.

But it can dramatically increase something more valuable:

**credibility.**

Clients remember the consultant who helped them avoid spending money unnecessarily.

And when a major investment eventually becomes necessary, that credibility matters.

## TRUST REQUIRES CANDOR

Clients don't need consultants who agree with everything they say.

They need people willing to tell them when something doesn't make sense.

That doesn't mean being argumentative.

It means being willing to explain:

- where assumptions may be weak
- where risks are being underestimated
- where additional information is needed
- where an alternative deserves consideration
- where the recommended path may create unintended consequences

Technical knowledge gives an engineer the ability to provide that advice.

Judgment determines when and how to provide it.

## THE BEST ENGINEERS MAKE DECISIONS EASIER

Senior municipal leaders are constantly balancing technical, financial, operational, regulatory, and political considerations.

Adding another 80-page technical memorandum to their workload isn't necessarily creating value.

A trusted advisor helps turn complexity into clarity.

Instead of simply presenting five alternatives, they explain the tradeoffs.

Instead of reporting a risk score, they explain what that risk means.

Instead of presenting another spreadsheet, they help answer:

**What should we do next, and why?**

That is where engineering begins to move beyond technical service.

It becomes decision support.

## TRUST COMPOUNDS

You don't become a trusted advisor because your business card says Principal, Director, or Vice President.

And you certainly don't become one by adding "Trusted Advisor" to a LinkedIn headline.

Clients decide whether you've earned that role.

It happens gradually.

One honest recommendation.

One difficult conversation handled well.

One problem identified before it became a crisis.

One phone call when there was nothing to sell.

Eventually, the relationship changes.

The client stops calling only when they need an engineering deliverable.

They start calling when they're trying to decide **what to do next**.

That's the relationship worth building.

>**Engineering delivers projects. Leadership builds lasting value.**
