# What Does a Risk Score Actually Mean?

Infrastructure asset management loves risk scores.

They give us a way to take complicated information about an asset and turn it into something that can be compared, ranked, mapped, and eventually used to support capital planning.

A water main receives a risk score of 18.

A pump station receives a 12.

Another asset receives a 24.

We sort the spreadsheet from highest to lowest, color the top scores red, and suddenly the priorities appear obvious.

But what does a risk score of 24 actually mean?

That question matters more than the number itself.

## A RISK SCORE IS A MODEL, NOT A MEASUREMENT

When we measure pressure in a water main, we can report it in psi.

When we measure flow, we can report it in gallons per minute.

When we measure the diameter of a pipe, we can report it in inches.

A risk score is different.

It isn't something we directly measure in the field.

It is the output of a model.

At its simplest, infrastructure risk is often represented as:

>**Risk = Probability of Failure × Consequence of Failure**

That framework is useful because it forces us to consider two very different questions:

**How likely is the asset to fail?**

and

**How serious would the consequences be if it did?**

But both sides of that equation usually contain assumptions.

And those assumptions matter.

## PROBABILITY OF FAILURE ISN'T ALWAYS A PROBABILITY

The phrase *probability of failure* sounds precise.

Sometimes it is.

If a utility has sufficient historical failure data, asset characteristics, inspection records, operating conditions, and deterioration models, it may be possible to estimate an actual probability of failure.

But many infrastructure programs don't have that level of data.

Instead, probability of failure may be represented by a score.

Perhaps:

1 = Very Low
2 = Low
3 = Moderate
4 = High
5 = Very High

The score might be based on factors such as:

* asset age
* material
* failure history
* condition assessments
* operating environment
* pressure
* maintenance history
* inspection results

That's still valuable.

But a probability-of-failure score of 4 doesn't necessarily mean the asset has an 80% chance of failing.

It means the asset meets whatever criteria the organization has defined as **High**.

That distinction is important.

## CONSEQUENCE OF FAILURE REQUIRES JUDGMENT TOO

Consequence can be even more complicated.

What happens when the asset fails?

For a water main, consequences might include:

* number of customers affected
* critical customers such as hospitals or emergency facilities
* roadway impacts
* environmental impacts
* repair costs
* service interruption
* available system redundancy
* regulatory consequences
* public safety
* reputational impacts

Some of those factors can be quantified fairly well.

Others require judgment.

Consider two water mains with similar condition.

One serves a residential neighborhood with multiple alternative supply paths.

The other crosses a major highway and provides critical service to a hospital.

Their physical condition may be nearly identical.

Their consequences of failure are not.

That's precisely why risk analysis is more useful than condition alone.

But it also means the resulting risk score depends heavily on how the organization defines consequence.

## THE SAME SCORE CAN REPRESENT VERY DIFFERENT RISKS

Suppose a utility uses a simple 5 × 5 risk matrix.

An asset with:

**Probability of Failure = 5**
**Consequence of Failure = 2**

receives a risk score of:

**10**

Another asset with:

**Probability of Failure = 2**
**Consequence of Failure = 5**

also receives:

**10**

Mathematically, they are identical.

Operationally, they may represent completely different problems.

The first may be an asset that fails frequently but causes relatively minor disruption.

The second may be unlikely to fail, but failure could have severe consequences.

Should they receive the same management response?

Probably not.

One might justify routine rehabilitation or replacement.

The other might justify redundancy, emergency planning, additional monitoring, or targeted inspection even if immediate replacement isn't warranted.

The score helps identify risk.

It doesn't tell us what to do about it.

## RISK SCORES ARE MOST USEFUL FOR COMPARISON

This is where I think risk scores provide their greatest value.

A risk score doesn't necessarily tell us that an asset is "safe" or "unsafe."

It helps us compare competing infrastructure needs using a consistent framework.

That becomes extremely valuable when a utility has hundreds or thousands of assets competing for limited capital.

Instead of asking:

**Which assets are oldest?**

or:

**Which assets are in the worst condition?**

we can begin asking:

**Which assets represent the greatest risk to the system?**

That's a much better question.

But even then, the ranking should be the beginning of the analysis, not the end.

## THE MODEL IS ONLY AS GOOD AS ITS ASSUMPTIONS

Risk models can look remarkably sophisticated once they are inside a dashboard.

There are maps.

There are rankings.

There are colors.

There may even be enough decimal places to make everyone feel particularly scientific.

But precision in the output doesn't guarantee accuracy in the model.

If consequence scores are poorly defined, the ranking will reflect that.

If condition data is outdated, the ranking will reflect that.

If failure history is incomplete, the ranking will reflect that.

If every asset of a certain age receives the same probability-of-failure score regardless of operating conditions, the ranking will reflect that too.

Analytics can process assumptions very efficiently.

It cannot make bad assumptions good.

## RISK SCORES SHOULD SUPPORT ENGINEERING JUDGMENT

This doesn't make risk scoring less valuable.

It makes understanding the model more important.

A well-designed risk framework can help utilities:

* identify vulnerabilities
* compare very different infrastructure needs
* prioritize inspections
* evaluate rehabilitation strategies
* identify where redundancy could reduce consequences
* support capital planning
* communicate infrastructure needs to leadership

But the score should never become a substitute for understanding the system.

When an asset appears near the top of a risk ranking, the next question shouldn't automatically be:

**When do we replace it?**

The better questions are:

**Why is the score high?**

**What is driving the risk?**

**How confident are we in the underlying data?**

**And what action would actually reduce that risk?**

Sometimes the answer will be replacement.

Sometimes it will be rehabilitation.

Sometimes it will be additional inspection.

Sometimes improving redundancy will accomplish more than replacing the asset.

And sometimes the analysis will reveal that the score itself needs another look.

## THE NUMBER ISN'T THE DECISION

Risk scoring gives infrastructure owners something extremely valuable: a structured way to organize uncertainty.

But the number isn't the risk.

And the number isn't the decision.

It's a simplified representation of what we know, what we assume, and what we believe matters.

The real value comes from understanding what is behind the score and using that information to make better decisions.

Because ultimately, infrastructure asset management isn't about creating the perfect risk score.

**It's about using imperfect information to make better decisions about real infrastructure.**

>Engineering begins with calculations.
>Infrastructure analytics powers better decisions.
