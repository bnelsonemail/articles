# A Cost Estimate Is Not a Prediction

Early in a project, someone inevitably wants to know:

**How much is this going to cost?**

It is a reasonable question. Owners need numbers to establish budgets, evaluate alternatives, secure funding, and decide whether a project should move forward.

Engineers respond by developing an opinion of probable construction cost based on the information available at the time.

The problem begins when that estimate quietly becomes something it was never intended to be:

**a prediction.**

## The Number Looks More Certain Than It Is

Suppose an early estimate puts a project at $12.4 million.

That number looks remarkably precise.

But underneath it may be assumptions about quantities, unit prices, site conditions, construction methods, permitting requirements, escalation, schedule, and contingency.

At an early stage of design, some of those assumptions may be supported by good information.

Others are simply the best information available at the time.

That distinction matters.

As the project develops, quantities become better defined. Geotechnical information becomes available. Utility conflicts are identified. Permitting requirements become clearer. Equipment selections change. Market conditions move. The construction schedule develops.

The estimate changes because our **information changes**.

That does not necessarily mean the earlier estimate was wrong.

It means it was an estimate.

## An Estimate Should Tell Us What We Know

A useful estimate does more than produce a number.

It should help us understand:

* What information is currently available?
* What assumptions have been made?
* Where does significant uncertainty remain?
* Which assumptions could materially affect the result?
* How much contingency is appropriate at this stage?
* What information would most improve the estimate?

That last question is particularly important.

If additional analysis will not materially affect the decision, obtaining more information may have little value.

But if one uncertain assumption could change the preferred alternative or determine whether the project is financially feasible, reducing that uncertainty may be extremely valuable.

The purpose of estimating is therefore not simply to calculate a number.

It is to support a decision.

## Forecasting Has the Same Problem

This idea extends beyond engineering.

I have been spending more time studying financial modeling and forecasting, and one similarity has become increasingly apparent.

A financial forecast is also built from assumptions.

Revenue growth. Labor costs. Inflation. Hiring. Interest rates. Customer demand. Capital expenditures.

Change the assumptions and the forecast changes.

That does not make forecasting useless.

Quite the opposite.

A good forecast gives management a structured way to say:

**Given what we know today and the assumptions we are making, this is what we currently expect.**

More importantly, it gives us something against which actual results can be evaluated.

When reality differs from the forecast, the useful question is not simply:

**Why was the forecast wrong?**

It is:

**What changed?**

Was an assumption incorrect?

Did a condition change?

Did something occur that could not reasonably have been known?

Or is the new information telling us that our expectations for the future should change?

Engineers ask versions of these questions throughout project development.

Businesses should too.

## Precision Is Not the Same as Accuracy

Modern tools make it very easy to produce impressive-looking numbers.

A spreadsheet can calculate a project cost to the penny.

A financial model can forecast revenue twelve months into the future to the nearest dollar.

Neither means we know the future with that level of precision.

The calculation may be precise.

The assumptions underneath it are still uncertain.

Recognizing that uncertainty is not a weakness in the analysis.

It is part of understanding what the analysis actually means.

## Engineering Judgment

A cost estimate is not a promise about what a project will cost.

It is a structured assessment based on the information available at a particular point in time.

As better information becomes available, the estimate should change.

The same principle applies to forecasts, schedules, financial models, and many other tools we use to support decisions.

The goal is not to predict the future perfectly.

We cannot.

The goal is to understand what we know, identify what we do not know, make reasonable assumptions, and update our decisions as the evidence changes.

That is not uncertainty undermining the analysis.

**That is judgment being applied to uncertainty.**

>Engineering begins with calculations.
>Better decisions require judgment.
