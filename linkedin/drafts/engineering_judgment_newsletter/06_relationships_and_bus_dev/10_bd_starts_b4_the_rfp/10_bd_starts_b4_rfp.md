# Business Development Starts Before the RFP

When an RFP is released, it can feel like the beginning of a business development effort.

The opportunity appears.

The team gets assembled.

The proposal strategy meeting gets scheduled.

Resumes are updated. Project sheets are selected. Someone starts building the org chart.

But in many cases, the most important business development work should have happened long before the RFP was ever published.

**Business development starts before the opportunity exists.**

## CLIENTS DON'T START THINKING ABOUT PROJECTS WHEN THE RFP IS RELEASED

Infrastructure projects rarely appear overnight.

A utility may recognize a capacity problem years before improvements are funded.

Operators may have been dealing with reliability issues for months.

A capital planning team may already know that a facility needs rehabilitation.

Growth projections may be pointing toward a future expansion.

Regulatory requirements may be approaching.

By the time an RFP reaches the street, the client has often been thinking about the underlying problem for quite some time.

The firms that understand that problem early have an advantage.

Not because they know what the RFP will say.

Because they understand **why the project exists.**

## BUSINESS DEVELOPMENT ISN'T JUST PURSUIT MANAGEMENT

Engineers sometimes associate business development with proposals, presentations, conferences, and client lunches.

Those things have their place.

But some of the most valuable business development happens during ordinary project work.

It happens when you ask an operator what problems are creating headaches.

It happens when you understand a utility's capital program instead of only the project you're currently designing.

It happens when you notice that a recurring operational problem may eventually require a capital solution.

It happens when you help a client think through an issue even when there isn't an immediate project attached to it.

Those conversations build something more valuable than an opportunity pipeline.

They build **understanding and trust.**

## KNOW THE CLIENT BEFORE YOU KNOW THE PROJECT

If your first serious conversation about a client's needs happens after the RFP is released, you're already trying to learn under a deadline.

You need to understand:

* What pressures is the organization facing?
* What matters to operations?
* What capital investments are coming?
* Where are they experiencing reliability problems?
* What regulatory or growth pressures are emerging?
* What has worked well on previous projects?
* What hasn't?

None of that requires knowing the scope of the next RFP.

It requires knowing the client.

That distinction matters.

Because when a future opportunity does emerge, you're not starting with:

**"How do we win this project?"**

You're starting with:

**"We understand why this project matters."**

## THE BEST BUSINESS DEVELOPMENT OFTEN LOOKS LIKE GOOD CLIENT SERVICE

There is a temptation to think of business development as a separate activity from engineering.

I don't think it is.

A project manager who identifies a problem before it becomes urgent is doing business development.

An engineer who listens carefully to operators is doing business development.

A technical specialist who explains an emerging technology without immediately trying to sell a project is doing business development.

A project team that delivers what it promised is definitely doing business development.

Every interaction influences whether the client wants to work with that firm again.

That means business development isn't something that begins when marketing opens a pursuit number.

It's happening throughout the relationship.

## DON'T CHASE PROJECTS. BUILD RELATIONSHIPS.

There will always be opportunities where a firm sees an RFP for the first time and decides to pursue it.

Sometimes those pursuits will be successful.

But sustainable growth can't depend entirely on reacting to procurement notices.

Strong client relationships are built over years, not during a three-week proposal window.

That means understanding the client's system.

Understanding their people.

Understanding their priorities.

And being useful before there is something to sell.

When the RFP eventually arrives, the proposal still matters.

The team matters.

The technical approach matters.

The interview matters.

But the business development process didn't begin when the RFP was posted.

**It began with every interaction that came before it.**
