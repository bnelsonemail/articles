# Engineering Judgment Article Scopes

## Article 1: Should We Design for Flow Reversal That May Never Happen?

### Topic and Strong Angle

Turn the recent force-main restraint discussion into an article about designing for credible future operating conditions.

**“Should We Design for Flow Reversal That May Never Happen?”**

A force main may currently operate in one direction, but future interconnections, emergency operations, pump-station changes, or system reconfiguration could reverse that flow. The design question is not simply whether reversal is physically possible. It is whether that future condition is credible enough to justify additional restraints, valves, analysis, and construction cost today.

### Central Argument

> Good engineering does not design for every imaginable future—it identifies which future conditions are credible enough to influence today’s design.

This article combines pipeline design, reliability, lifecycle cost, capital stewardship, and engineering judgment without repeating recent articles about risk scores, probabilistic cost estimates, or ARV placement.

### Concise Outline

#### 1. The Design Assumption Hidden in Plain Sight

- Current flow direction is shown or communicated.
- Restraint and valve decisions are developed around that condition.
- The drawings may never state whether reversal was evaluated.

#### 2. How Flow Could Change

- Temporary emergency pumping
- Future system interconnections
- Pump-station modifications
- Valve reconfiguration
- Changes in system demand or operating strategy

#### 3. Possible Responses

- Design only for the documented current condition.
- Provide full bidirectional capability.
- Protect selected critical locations while avoiding wholesale overdesign.
- Preserve space or connections for a future modification.

#### 4. Evaluate Credibility—not Mere Possibility

- Is reversal hydraulically feasible?
- Is it included in a master plan?
- Would existing pumps and valves support it?
- What is the consequence if reversal occurs without suitable restraint?
- What is the incremental cost of accommodating it now?

#### 5. Document the Decision

- Basis of design
- Operating condition evaluated
- Future conditions considered
- Owner confirmation
- Limitations that operations personnel need to understand

#### 6. Engineering-Judgment Takeaway

- Unexamined assumptions become future surprises.
- Explicit assumptions allow the owner to choose deliberately between resilience and cost.

### Drafting Task

Write **600–800 words covering Sections 1, 2, 4, and 6**. Keep this first version conceptual.

#### Hypothetical Opening

> A force main is designed to convey flow from west to east. Joint restraint is therefore developed for the anticipated thrust conditions. During review, someone asks a simple question: What happens if the utility reverses the flow someday?

#### Required Decision Table

| Question | Evidence to review |
| --- | --- |
| Is reverse flow physically possible? | System connectivity and hydraulic grade |
| Is reversal operationally plausible? | Utility procedures and emergency plans |
| Is reversal anticipated? | Master plans and planned improvements |
| What happens if it occurs? | Thrust, restraint, valve, surge, and capacity review |
| What would accommodating it cost now? | Incremental materials and construction effort |
| Can it be addressed later? | Access, shutdown requirements, and retrofit difficulty |

#### Required Closing

> The right answer may still be to design for one-way operation. The important thing is that the limitation is understood, documented, and accepted—not discovered during an emergency twenty years later.

## Article 2: The Most Expensive Design Errors Live Between Disciplines

### Topic and Strong Angle

Write about multidisciplinary coordination as a reliability and cost-control practice—not merely a QA/QC exercise.

**“The Most Expensive Design Errors Live Between Disciplines”**

On municipal facility projects, each discipline’s drawings can be technically correct while the combined design still contains serious problems. A structural opening may not fit the mechanical equipment. Electrical loads may reflect an earlier pump selection. Instrumentation requirements may not appear in the equipment specification. Civil grading may conflict with equipment access or generator placement.

### Central Argument

> Discipline quality is necessary, but project reliability is created at the interfaces.

This article draws from real project leadership rather than textbook design. It broadens the body of work beyond probabilistic cost estimating and force-main details while retaining the themes of reliability, risk, lifecycle performance, and avoiding expensive late-stage corrections.

### Concise Outline

#### 1. How Individually Correct Drawings Produce a Flawed Project

- Each discipline reviews its own work.
- Changes do not always propagate through the full design.
- No single sheet reveals the entire conflict.

#### 2. Common Municipal-Project Interfaces

- Mechanical equipment versus structural openings and foundations
- Pump selections versus electrical loads and generator capacity
- Instrumentation versus controls narratives and specifications
- Yard piping versus grading, drainage, and equipment access
- Process requirements versus startup and testing procedures

#### 3. Why Interface Problems Are Expensive

- They emerge during submittals, fabrication, or construction.
- Corrections may require redesign, change orders, delays, or compromised access.
- The operating staff inherits whatever cannot be corrected economically.

#### 4. A Better Combined-Design Review

- Review systems and operating scenarios—not just sheets.
- Track key design inputs across disciplines.
- Assign ownership for resolving each interface.
- Verify that late design changes reached every affected document.

#### 5. What a Project Manager or EOR Should Ask

- What changed?
- Who else depends on that information?
- Where must the change appear?
- How will the facility be constructed, started, operated, and maintained?

#### 6. Engineering-Judgment Takeaway

- A thick redline set does not prove a good review.
- A good review finds the few coordination failures capable of changing cost, schedule, safety, or performance.

### Drafting Task

Spend one evening writing **700–900 words covering Sections 1–3 and 6**. Stay conceptual and anonymize the examples.

#### Required Opening

> Some of the most expensive design errors are not contained within a civil, structural, mechanical, or electrical drawing. They exist in the space between them.

#### Required Interface Table

| Design decision | Potential downstream effect |
| --- | --- |
| Pump selection changes | Electrical load, generator size, controls, piping and foundation |
| Wet-well geometry changes | Structural design, pump layout, access and excavation |
| Equipment location changes | Grading, drainage, conduit, piping and maintenance access |
| Control requirement changes | Instrument list, panels, programming and startup testing |
| Pipe alignment changes | Structures, utilities, restraints, access and permitting |
