# Engineering Judgment – Article Series

## Series Objective

Explore how engineering judgment, project delivery, economics, and financial analysis intersect when making capital decisions.

Each article begins with concepts grounded in practical engineering and project experience, then expands into broader analytical and financial decision-making principles.

The progression is intentional:

**Estimating → Uncertainty → Variance Analysis → Capital Economics**

---

# 1. A Cost Estimate Is Not a Prediction

## Core Idea

A project cost estimate represents the best assessment that can be made using the information available at a particular point in time. It should not be interpreted as a precise prediction of the final construction cost.

## Engineering Perspective

An engineering cost estimate may incorporate:

* Estimated quantities
* Historical unit prices
* Recent bid data
* Vendor or contractor information
* Design maturity
* Construction contingencies
* Market conditions
* Known project risks
* Assumptions about future conditions

Even a carefully prepared estimate contains uncertainty.

As design progresses, quantities become better defined, unknown conditions are investigated, and assumptions are replaced with actual information.

The estimate should therefore evolve as the project evolves.

## Analytical Connection

The same principle applies to business and financial forecasting.

A forecast is not valuable because it predicts the future perfectly.

A useful forecast:

* Makes assumptions explicit
* Establishes reasonable expectations
* Identifies important uncertainties
* Provides a baseline for comparison
* Helps management respond when actual results differ from expectations

## Key Question

**If an estimate is not a prediction, what should decision-makers expect it to tell them?**

# 2. The Problem With Point Estimates

## Core Idea

A single number can create a false sense of certainty.

A project may have an estimated cost of $10 million, but that number alone says little about the uncertainty surrounding the estimate.

## Engineering Perspective

Project outcomes can be affected by changes in:

* Quantities
* Material prices
* Labor costs
* Site conditions
* Permitting requirements
* Construction schedules
* Market competition
* Scope
* Design assumptions

Rather than asking only:

> What will the project cost?

A better set of questions may include:

> What assumptions drive the estimate?

> Which assumptions have the greatest effect on cost?

> What happens if those assumptions change?

## Analytical Connection

This leads naturally to:

* Sensitivity analysis
* Scenario analysis
* Ranges
* Contingency analysis
* Probabilistic analysis

Instead of relying exclusively on a single expected outcome, decision-makers can evaluate how results change under different conditions.

For example:

| Scenario  | Estimated Cost |
| --------- | -------------: |
| Favorable |   $9.2 million |
| Base      |  $10.0 million |
| Adverse   |  $11.4 million |

The purpose is not to create three competing predictions.

The purpose is to understand the range of reasonable outcomes and the assumptions responsible for them.

## Key Question

**When does presenting a single number communicate more certainty than the underlying analysis actually supports?**

# 3. What Does "On Budget" Actually Tell Us?

## Core Idea

Knowing that a project is over or under budget identifies a result. It does not explain the result.

## Engineering Perspective

Suppose a project is 8% over budget.

That variance could result from:

* Quantity growth
* Scope changes
* Material escalation
* Labor costs
* Schedule delays
* Owner-directed changes
* Differing site conditions
* Permitting requirements
* Estimating assumptions
* Design changes

Simply reporting the variance does not identify which of these factors occurred.

## Analytical Connection

The basic relationship is simple:

**Variance = Actual Result − Expected Result**

But calculating the variance is only the beginning of the analysis.

The more useful questions are:

* What caused the variance?
* Is it temporary or permanent?
* Was it controllable?
* Was the original assumption reasonable?
* Is the same variance likely to continue?
* Does the forecast need to change?
* Does management need to take action?

This is where project-performance analysis and financial variance analysis begin to converge.

The objective is not simply to report that actual performance differed from expectations.

The objective is to understand **why**.

## Key Question

**If management knows that a project is 8% over budget but does not know what caused the variance, how much useful information does the 8% actually provide?**

# 4. Why the Lowest-Cost Alternative Isn't Always the Best Value

## Core Idea

The alternative with the lowest initial construction cost is not necessarily the alternative with the lowest long-term economic cost.

## Engineering Perspective

Consider two technically viable alternatives:

|              | Alternative A | Alternative B |
| ------------ | ------------: | ------------: |
| Initial Cost |  $5.0 million |  $5.7 million |

Looking only at initial capital cost makes Alternative A appear preferable.

But the decision may change after considering:

* Useful life
* Operations and maintenance costs
* Energy consumption
* Rehabilitation requirements
* Replacement cycles
* Reliability
* Residual value
* Future capital expenditures

A more complete comparison considers costs and benefits occurring throughout the life of the asset.

## Financial Connection

Because those cash flows occur at different points in time, they cannot always be compared directly.

This introduces concepts such as:

* Time value of money
* Discount rates
* Present value
* Net present value
* Equivalent annual cost
* Lifecycle cost analysis
* Opportunity cost
* Sensitivity analysis

The objective is not simply to determine:

> Which alternative costs less today?

The more useful question is:

> Which alternative provides the best economic value over the period that matters to the organization?

## Broader Capital-Allocation Connection

This becomes even more important when an organization has multiple worthwhile projects competing for limited capital.

A technically justified project is not automatically the best use of the next available dollar.

Engineering feasibility establishes whether an alternative **can** work.

Economic and financial analysis helps determine whether the alternative represents a **good use of limited resources**.

## Key Question

**How often does focusing on initial project cost cause us to overlook the economics of owning and operating the asset for the next 20, 30, or 50 years?**

# Series Progression

The four articles build on one another:

1. **A Cost Estimate Is Not a Prediction**
   Establishes uncertainty and the purpose of forecasting.

2. **The Problem With Point Estimates**
   Introduces sensitivity and scenario analysis.

3. **What Does "On Budget" Actually Tell Us?**
   Moves from reporting results to understanding performance drivers.

4. **Why the Lowest-Cost Alternative Isn't Always the Best Value**
   Extends the discussion into lifecycle economics and capital allocation.

Together, the articles explore a broader theme:

> **Good engineering decisions require more than technical calculations. They require understanding uncertainty, identifying the drivers of performance, evaluating economic consequences, and exercising judgment when the answer is not obvious.**
