# How Engineers Become Trusted Advisors

Engineering consulting is built around solving problems.

A client identifies a need. Engineers evaluate the problem, develop alternatives, prepare designs, obtain permits, support construction, and ultimately help deliver a solution.

Technical competence is the foundation of that work.

But technical competence alone does not create the strongest client relationships.

Over time, some engineers move beyond being the person a client calls when they need a project designed. They become someone the client calls **before they have decided what the project should be**.

That is a very different relationship.

The engineer is no longer simply providing a technical service.

They have become a **trusted advisor**.

## Technical Expertise Is the Starting Point

There is no shortcut around technical credibility.

Clients need to know that an engineer understands the infrastructure, can recognize risk, and can provide technically sound recommendations.

But as engineers become more experienced, technical ability increasingly becomes the expectation rather than the differentiator.

Most experienced engineers can calculate flows, evaluate pump curves, review condition assessments, develop design alternatives, and prepare construction documents.

The more interesting question becomes:

**Why does a client call one engineer before calling another?**

Often, it is because that engineer understands more than the technical problem.

They understand the client's world.

## Understand the Problem Behind the Project

Municipal infrastructure decisions rarely exist in isolation.

Consider something as straightforward as replacing an aging pump station.

From an engineering perspective, the questions might involve:

- capacity and future demand
- equipment condition
- system hydraulics
- redundancy and reliability
- electrical and controls
- constructability and phasing

But the utility director may be thinking about an entirely different set of questions:

- Can the project fit within the next capital budget?
- Is funding available?
- Does another project need to happen first?
- Can operations maintain service during construction?
- Will rates need to increase?
- Is there a regulatory deadline?
- How will the project be explained to elected officials?
- What happens if the utility waits another five years?

The engineering analysis matters.

But it exists within a much larger decision.

Trusted advisors learn to understand that larger context.

Instead of beginning with:

**"What do you want us to design?"**

they begin with:

**"What are you trying to accomplish?"**

That question can completely change the solution.

## Sometimes the Best Recommendation Is Not a Project

Consulting firms exist to perform work. That economic reality isn't particularly mysterious.

But becoming a trusted advisor sometimes requires recommending a solution that produces **less work for the consultant**.

The right recommendation might be:

- monitoring an asset rather than immediately replacing it
- rehabilitating rather than replacing
- making an operational change that reduces risk
- coordinating improvements with another planned capital project
- deferring an investment until better information is available

In those situations, the best recommendation might simply be:

**Don't build it yet.**

That advice may reduce today's engineering fee.

But it can create something far more valuable over the long term:

**credibility.**

Clients remember who helped them spend money wisely.

They also remember who appeared interested primarily in creating the next project.

## Trust Is Built Between Projects

Business development is often associated with pursuits, proposals, interviews, and contracts.

But strong client relationships frequently develop long before any of those things happen.

An engineer might share something learned while working with another utility.

They might alert a client to an upcoming regulatory requirement.

They might send information about a funding program.

They might make an introduction to someone who can solve a problem their own firm cannot.

Or they might simply call because they remember the client was dealing with an issue several months earlier.

None of those interactions necessarily generates immediate revenue.

That is precisely why they can be so powerful.

They demonstrate that the relationship isn't entirely transactional.

**You're paying attention to the client's success, not simply the client's next procurement.**

Over time, those interactions compound.

## Trusted Advisors Are Willing to Disagree

Trust does not mean telling clients what they want to hear.

In fact, sometimes it requires exactly the opposite.

An engineer may need to explain that:

- an assumption is too optimistic
- a preferred alternative carries more risk than the client realizes
- additional investigation is needed before proceeding
- a proposed schedule is unrealistic
- the least expensive alternative may create greater lifecycle cost or operational risk

Those conversations aren't always comfortable.

But avoiding them doesn't serve the client.

Technical expertise gives engineers the ability to identify these issues.

**Professional judgment gives them the responsibility to raise them.**

The goal isn't to win an argument.

The goal is to help the client make a better decision.

## Translate Complexity Into Decisions

Engineering naturally produces information:

- hydraulic and process models
- technical reports
- cost estimates
- condition assessments
- risk matrices
- alternatives analyses
- schedules
- spreadsheets

All of those tools can be valuable. But information alone does not necessarily make a decision easier.

A municipal leader doesn't always need another 80-page technical memorandum describing five alternatives in exquisite detail.

They need to understand the tradeoffs:

- What does each alternative cost?
- What risk does it reduce?
- What new risks does it introduce?
- What happens if the project is deferred?
- Which assumptions matter most?
- What would cause the recommendation to change?

And ultimately:

**What should we do next, and why?**

One of the most valuable skills an experienced engineer can develop is the ability to turn technical complexity into decision clarity.

That is where engineering analysis becomes **decision support**.

## Understand What the Client Values

Engineers naturally tend to evaluate projects through technical measures:

- reliability
- capacity
- risk
- performance
- constructability

Those measures matter, but clients may define value more broadly.

Different people within the same organization may also view the same investment differently:

- **Public works director:** predictable project delivery
- **Utility director:** operational reliability and simplicity
- **Finance director:** affordability and long-term financial sustainability
- **City manager:** organizational priorities and overall capital needs
- **Elected officials:** community impact and responsible use of public funds

The same infrastructure decision can therefore look very different depending on who is sitting at the table.

Trusted advisors learn to recognize those perspectives without abandoning sound engineering judgment.

They connect the technical recommendation to the outcomes that matter to the organization.

## Think Beyond the Current Assignment

One of the biggest differences between transactional consulting and advisory relationships is time horizon.

A project-focused engineer naturally asks:

**How do we successfully deliver this project?**

A trusted advisor also asks:

**How does this project fit into where the client is going?**

A pipeline replacement may affect a future roadway project.

A pump station improvement may influence long-term system expansion.

A treatment upgrade may affect the utility's capital plan for the next decade.

A development decision today may create infrastructure obligations years later.

Understanding those connections allows engineers to help clients make decisions that remain sensible beyond the immediate project.

That perspective becomes increasingly valuable as infrastructure systems, capital programs, and regulatory requirements become more complex.

## Trust Is Earned Incrementally

Nobody becomes a trusted advisor because their title changes from Project Manager to Principal, Director, or Vice President.

And putting **Trusted Advisor** in a LinkedIn headline does not trigger some obscure professional transformation.

Clients decide whether an engineer has earned that position.

It happens gradually:

- one honest recommendation
- one difficult conversation handled professionally
- one problem identified before it became a crisis
- one commitment kept when circumstances became difficult
- one phone call when there was nothing to sell

Eventually, something changes.

The client stops calling only when they need an engineering deliverable.

They start calling when they are considering a problem.

Sometimes they call before they fully understand what the problem is.

And eventually they ask:

**"What do you think we should do?"**

That may be one of the strongest indicators of trust an engineer can receive.

Because at that point, the engineer is no longer simply designing infrastructure.

They are helping the client decide **what comes next**.

>**Engineering delivers projects. Leadership builds lasting value.**
