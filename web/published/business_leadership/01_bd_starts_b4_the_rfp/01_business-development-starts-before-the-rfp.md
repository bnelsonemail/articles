# Business Development Starts Before the RFP

When an RFP is released, it can feel like the beginning of a business development effort.

The opportunity appears in the procurement system. A go/no-go decision is made. The pursuit team is assembled. Marketing begins collecting resumes and project sheets. Someone develops a proposal schedule, and the team starts discussing differentiators, technical approaches, and interview strategy.

Those activities matter.

But they are **pursuit activities**.

The business development process should have started much earlier.

In municipal infrastructure, projects rarely appear suddenly. A utility does not discover that it needs a new pump station on the same morning it publishes an RFP. A treatment facility does not unexpectedly reach capacity because someone opened a procurement portal. A deteriorating pipeline, recurring operational problem, regulatory requirement, or growth constraint usually exists long before professional services are procured.

Sometimes the underlying need has been developing for years.

That creates an important distinction:

**An RFP may begin the pursuit, but it should not begin the relationship.**

## THE PROJECT EXISTS BEFORE THE PROCUREMENT

Consider how an infrastructure project actually develops.

An operator notices that a pump is running longer than it used to.

A maintenance team sees recurring failures in a piece of equipment.

Development increases demand within part of the service area.

A hydraulic model identifies a future capacity constraint.

A regulatory requirement creates the need for treatment improvements.

An asset management program identifies infrastructure approaching the end of its useful life.

A capital improvement plan identifies a project several years before design funding becomes available.

None of these begins with an RFP.

They begin with a **problem, constraint, risk, or opportunity**.

By the time professional engineering services are formally advertised, the organization may have already spent considerable time discussing the issue internally. Operations, engineering, finance, management, regulators, and elected officials may all have influenced how the eventual project is defined.

A consulting firm that begins learning about the client only after the RFP is published therefore starts with a significant knowledge gap.

The more important question isn't:

**What does the RFP say?**

It is:

**Why does this project need to exist in the first place?**

Understanding that requires knowing much more than the procurement document.

## KNOW THE CLIENT'S SYSTEM, NOT JUST YOUR PROJECT

One of the easiest traps in consulting engineering is becoming very knowledgeable about the project you're currently designing while knowing relatively little about the client's broader system.

You may understand every detail of a force main replacement while knowing almost nothing about the utility's five-year capital program.

You may be designing improvements at one lift station without knowing which other stations are experiencing capacity or reliability problems.

You may understand the technical requirements of a treatment upgrade without understanding the growth projections driving the investment.

Delivering the current project well is important.

But business leadership requires looking beyond the limits of the current scope.

That means gradually understanding things such as:

- the client's capital improvement program
- growth and capacity constraints
- recurring operational problems
- regulatory pressures
- asset condition and reliability concerns
- funding limitations
- planned development
- organizational priorities
- previous project experiences
- upcoming infrastructure investments

This doesn't mean turning every client conversation into a sales opportunity.

In fact, doing that is probably one of the quickest ways to weaken a client relationship.

The objective is to understand the organization well enough to recognize what matters to it.

## LISTEN TO THE PEOPLE CLOSEST TO THE PROBLEM

Some of the best business development intelligence will never appear in a capital improvement plan.

Operators know which equipment requires constant attention.

Maintenance personnel know which assets are becoming difficult to keep in service.

Project managers know where previous projects struggled.

Engineering staff know which improvements keep getting deferred.

Utility leadership knows where growth, funding, regulation, and public expectations are creating pressure.

These conversations matter because infrastructure problems rarely exist only on drawings or spreadsheets.

They exist in daily operations.

An engineer who asks good questions and listens carefully can begin to understand those problems long before anyone writes a scope of services.

More importantly, listening changes the nature of the relationship.

The engineer is no longer simply asking:

**What would you like us to design?**

The conversation becomes:

**What are you trying to accomplish?**

That is a much more valuable question.

## BUSINESS DEVELOPMENT SHOULD CREATE VALUE BEFORE THERE IS SOMETHING TO SELL

This may be the most important distinction.

Effective business development isn't simply identifying future projects before competitors do.

It is becoming useful before there is an immediate procurement opportunity.

That can happen in remarkably ordinary ways.

You might share an approach that worked on another project.

You might identify a potential operational concern during an unrelated discussion.

You might help a client understand the implications of an emerging technology.

You might connect someone with a technical specialist who can answer a difficult question.

You might notice that several seemingly unrelated problems point toward a larger system issue.

Sometimes the most valuable response may even be:

**You probably don't need a project yet.**

That answer may not create immediate revenue.

It can create something considerably more important.

**Credibility.**

Clients remember who helps them solve problems.

They also remember who seems to find a project hiding behind every conversation.

## THE BEST BUSINESS DEVELOPMENT OFTEN LOOKS LIKE GOOD CLIENT SERVICE

Business development is sometimes treated as something separate from project delivery.

There is project work over here.

Business development happens somewhere else.

In practice, the two are deeply connected.

Every successful project creates information about the client's needs.

Every project meeting creates an opportunity to understand priorities.

Every interaction with operations provides insight into how the infrastructure actually performs.

Every difficult project creates an opportunity to demonstrate how your firm responds when things do not go according to plan.

And every promise kept contributes to the client's perception of whether they want to work with you again.

A project manager who anticipates a problem is doing business development.

An engineer who communicates clearly with operations is doing business development.

A technical specialist who makes a complicated issue understandable is doing business development.

A team that delivers consistently is doing business development.

None of those activities requires a proposal.

They require good client service.

## FROM CONSULTANT TO TRUSTED ADVISOR

There is a difference between being a consultant a client hires and becoming an advisor a client trusts.

The first can happen with a contract.

The second takes time.

Trusted advisor status isn't created by a successful proposal or a good presentation. It develops through repeated interactions where the client learns that your advice is useful, your commitments mean something, and your recommendations are based on what is best for their organization rather than what creates the next assignment.

That trust is accumulated gradually.

You deliver what you promised.

You raise difficult issues early instead of waiting until they become problems.

You understand the client's constraints, including budget, operations, staffing, politics, schedule, and risk.

You are willing to recommend a smaller solution when a larger project isn't necessary.

Sometimes you tell the client something they may not want to hear.

And occasionally, the best advice you can give is that they don't need your services at all.

None of those interactions may produce immediate revenue.

But over time, they answer a much more important question for the client:

>Can I trust this person's judgment?

That is where the relationship begins to change.

Instead of calling only when a defined project needs engineering services, the client may begin calling earlier:

> We're considering this. 
> What do you think?

That conversation is fundamentally different.

You are no longer only responding to a scope.

You are helping the client think through the problem before the scope exists.

That is trusted advisor status.

And it cannot be manufactured during a three-week proposal period.

It has to be earned over time.

## UNDERSTANDING THE CAPITAL PROGRAM CHANGES THE CONVERSATION

For municipal infrastructure consultants, one of the most valuable sources of context is the client's capital program.

A capital improvement plan tells you more than which projects might eventually be advertised.

Taken together, planned investments can reveal where the organization is heading.

Several pump station projects may indicate broader capacity or reliability concerns.

Multiple pipeline replacements may point toward an aging portion of the system.

Treatment improvements may reflect changing regulations, growth, or source-water challenges.

A series of rehabilitation projects may indicate that maintaining existing infrastructure has become a strategic priority.

The objective should not be to scan the CIP looking for projects to chase.

The more useful exercise is asking:

**What story is this capital program telling us about the client's system?**

That question changes business development from opportunity tracking into strategic understanding.

## THERE IS A LINE BETWEEN POSITIONING AND PROCUREMENT

Pre-RFP business development also requires professional judgment.

Public-sector procurement exists for a reason.

Understanding a client's needs, developing relationships, discussing technical challenges, and providing useful expertise are not the same thing as attempting to influence a competitive procurement improperly.

Once a formal procurement process begins, communication rules may change. Firms need to understand and respect those boundaries.

That makes the objective of pre-RFP business development even clearer.

It isn't about obtaining inside information about an upcoming solicitation.

It is about developing legitimate knowledge of the client's organization, infrastructure, priorities, and challenges through ongoing professional relationships.

There is an enormous difference between:

**"What is going to be in the RFP?"**

and

**"What problems is this organization trying to solve?"**

The second question is where sustainable business development lives.

## DON'T BUILD A PIPELINE. BUILD CLIENT UNDERSTANDING.

Opportunity pipelines matter.

Forecasts matter.

Capture plans matter.

Go/no-go decisions matter.

Proposal strategy matters.

But those are tools.

The underlying business is still built on relationships between people and organizations.

A strong client relationship means understanding what success looks like from the client's perspective, not merely knowing which projects are coming.

It means knowing the system well enough to understand why investments are being made.

It means listening when there isn't an immediate opportunity.

It means delivering well when there is.

And it means being useful often enough that your firm becomes one of the organizations the client trusts when difficult problems arise.

When the RFP eventually arrives, the proposal still matters.

The team matters.

The technical approach matters.

The interview matters.

But by then, the most important question shouldn't be:

**How do we introduce ourselves to this client?**

Ideally, the client already knows the answer.

Business development didn't begin when the procurement notice appeared.

**It began with every interaction that came before it.**

> **Engineering delivers projects.**  
> **Leadership builds lasting value.**
